2025 was my most successful year attending conventions and demoing a game. Not for the wish lists, but for the actual feedback and ensuring the game is on the right course. Here's a rundown of how I have approached attending events to keep costs down and return on investment high.

I attended 3 different small local conventions. While reviewing my notes I can confirm that many of the issues early on have been addressed and that the game has steadily become more fun and engaging for players. Being able to track this progress is great for motivation and also makes sure that I keep focusing on the right next thing.

### Setting Your Goals

First off, get clear on what you want when you attend a convention. If you have too many or too unrealistic of goals, you can leave feeling disappointed. Leaving a convention should result in a boost of motivation to make progress on your game. I avoided goals focused on wish lists, marketing, etc... because you have too few of players to actually make much of a difference. 


My goals were:
1. Get high-quality testing feedback on the current state of the game
2. Get feedback on demographics most interested in the game
3. Meeting and observing other developers to generate more ideas

### First Step: Find the Right Conventions and Local Groups

You need to attend the right events depending on your goals. 

* Consider smaller local conventions. Sometimes these will have gaming areas that will have cheaper or even free placements for small local developers
* Find local organizations that support indie developers. In the Pacific Northwest we have Seattle Indies that frequently gets demonstration areas available 

### Getting Ready

- Trailer video
- Cards on hand
- QR Codes
- Posters over standing signs / banners

- Laptops with monitors


Focus on *listening* more than talking. 

### Demographic Testing and Getting People to Play

You've set up your booth, stickers, monitors, controllers and validated things are running it's time to get some players.

I do not worry about trying to have someone playing my game every second. Party games for example will almost always have someone playing at their game, but in talking with the developers, this almost never results in any boost of sales. So depending on your game, don't worry if there are periods where your game is not getting many plays. They will come and try it out.

This leads to an interesting opportunity: Seeing who is naturally interested in your game. Who gets drawn in? Who is willing to try? I might offer for people to play to help them sit down if they stop and are looking and thinking about, just to get over the convention / sit-down hurdle. But if you are spending your time shucking your game to everyone walking by, you will get a lot more players, but that's not how your Steam page is going to be working so you'll have no idea how well or who your game is drawing in.

I've noticed that people that hustle to get players frequently get people sitting down saying, "Well, I don't usually play this kind of game." and that's the problem. You are getting people that are not interested, taking up time from people that might be interested.

### Getting High Quality Playtesting Feedback

The most valuable piece of data you can get from conventions that you cannot get from anywhere else is watching people play your game. Emphasis on *watching*. 

1. If you are telling people how to play your game, you need to make the game more intuitive.
2. Do players get stuck? Do they get bored at certain points? 
3. What do players do when they first sit down and start playing? Do they go in an unexpected direction?
4. Do they have to spend a lot of time reading to get to the game? 

I will step in every once in a while to help out. Especially if it's younger player that might not have as much skill. But as much as possible, I try to stand back and observe
If you are attending multiple conventions, you should notice that these problems eventually give way to new problems. (Your game always has problems. It's ok.)

Sometimes I ask "Do you have any feedback?", but I found that got more superficial responses than observing the player or just waiting for them to share. 


### What Did I Learn?

By keeping notes after every convention, I've been able to review the struggles and joy that players experienced. Reviewing after every convention I am able to draw conclusions that: Yes, after six months the game is better than it used to be. And what more can you really hope for sometimes?

**Convention 1**
The game was pretty rough, with not a clear level up system and no tutorial. 


**Convention 2**
First tutorial attempt didn't go too well.
Changes to the level up system were well received and players enjoyed that


**Convention 3**
Players were able to complete the tutorial consistently.
Several players attempted multiple runs with different characters to try out the game
Players were able to progress from the tutorial, through the base screen, to more levels without any intervention
